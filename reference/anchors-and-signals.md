# Anchors and signals

What a first sentence may stand on, and which events count as a reason to
write now. The strategist assigns one anchor per campaign; the copywriter
writes to it; the audit reads a campaign by it.

## The three anchors

| Anchor | Stands on | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| **A, signal** | an event with a date | the tier 1 and tier 2 lists below |
| **B, data point** | a verifiable fact about the company now, plus what you compare it to | careers page, headcount, tech stack, public metrics |
| **C, segment insight** | a pattern true of a narrow segment, not of this company | knowing the segment |

Anchor B alone says nothing: "you have 8 engineers" is a database row.
The formula is the data point, the comparison, and what it usually means.

Anchor C is the default for most of any list and a legitimate track of
its own. The test: if the opening line could be pasted unchanged into a
message to the reader's direct competitor, the segment is too wide.

Never invent an anchor. No event and no verifiable fact means the message
goes on anchor C and says so. One campaign carries one anchor: in a mixed
campaign the signal contacts pull the average up and hide a segment track
that returns nothing.

## Signal tiers

**Tier 1, high intent.** An open SDR, BDR or sales role, usable about 30
days. A funding round, 30 to 90 days. A new decision maker in the role,
best inside 30 days. Headcount growing more than 20% a quarter. The
decision maker posted about the problem you solve, 24 to 72 hours.

**Tier 2, medium.** A key tool changed, 1 to 3 months. The decision maker
changed jobs 3 to 12 months ago. A competitor is their customer. They
spoke at a conference on your topic. They won a tender.

**Tier 3.** Firmographic fit only. A track of its own, never a filler
mixed into a signal campaign.

## The hiring question

Ask "who is being hired?" before calling a vacancy a signal. It is a
signal only when they are hiring the buyer. An agency hiring a PPC
specialist is building in-house; a company hiring a Head of Marketing is
about to rewrite its strategy.

A role open ninety days or more is a different thing: a stuck hire. That
is anchor B, a data point with a comparison, not a tier 1 signal.

### Reading a job board

Measured on the two largest boards of one country: a month of postings for
non-technical roles, about 10,000 vacancies from 5,700 employers.

- **The age of a posting, without history.** Most boards number postings in
  order and show the date of the last refresh, not of the first posting. An
  old number under a fresh date is a role kept open or reposted. Was: wait
  for a month of daily snapshots before calling a hire stuck. Now: read the
  age from the number on day one; the snapshots (board, posting number,
  company, normalised title, text fingerprint, first and last seen) confirm
  it later.
- **Open for long is not stuck.** About six in ten active postings were older
  than a month: field sales reps, accountants of retail chains and other
  evergreen roles stay up for years. Count as stuck only a senior or rare
  role with an old number and, once the snapshots exist, the same title at
  the same company still up after thirty days.
- **The size a company gives on a board is self-reported and often wrong**:
  an agricultural group listed as "under 20 people" had 16 openings. Check
  headcount on LinkedIn, and for "no HR department" the number of HR people
  there, before a small-company signal goes into a list.
- **Drop the employers that are not buyers before spending anything.**
  Recruiting agencies: the board's own agency mark misses some, so add words
  in the company name, "for our client" in the text, an AI check and a
  stop-list that grows. Government bodies, job centres and military units
  were about one employer in twelve; a job centre holding hundreds of
  postings looks exactly like "a company with many openings".
- **Tech companies hiring is not tech hiring.** A service that fills sales,
  marketing, finance or HR roles keeps tech companies as buyers and drops
  only the technical postings (engineering, QA, DevOps, design).
- **From a local-language board the decision-maker is on LinkedIn for about
  four companies in ten** (20 employers across niches: a company page for
  12, a senior person in the country for 8). Small distributors and
  manufacturers with a native-script name fail most - no page, or a page
  with one to five people - and the name in Latin script found three pages
  the native spelling did not. A website was listed for six employers in
  ten. Plan email by the website domain as the second channel from the
  start, and put it in the proposal as two channels, not as a gap.

Write to the person who decided to hire, not to the person who posted the
vacancy. Job posts in chats and boards are usually published by a recruiter,
often a contractor, who neither buys nor knows who owns the budget. Was:
reply to the recruiter under the post. Now: name the company (an application
form or a careers link often gives it away when the post does not), find the
founder or head of sales, and write to them with the vacancy as the reason.
If you sell the work that role does, offer both paths in one message: do it
with you, or help them hire well. Someone already hiring will not drop the
plan because a vendor asked.

## Public requests as a source

"Show me who asked for this last week" is a fair ask, but for a specialist
service sold to companies, public posts rarely answer it. Measured over one
week for a marketplace-advertising agency, using exact phrases and no AI
filter, on X (268 posts), Threads (195), the seller communities on Reddit
(about 2,400 posts and comments) and LinkedIn post search: zero companies
in the target countries wrote "we are looking for an agency or specialist".
What matched instead were freelancers advertising themselves, agencies
advertising, and agencies hiring staff.

The buyers were on the job boards. One page of last week's job postings for
the same skill in one country listed about nine brands hiring that role in
house, against five agencies. A brand opening that role has the budget and
the gap right now, and the hiring question above applies: drop the agencies
and recruiters, then write to the person who owns the hire.

Was: search social posts first and report what turned up. Now: for a
company-to-company service, start from brands posting the role on job
boards and use social posts as colour. Social search earns its place where
people do ask in public: freelance work in local-language communities, where
a week of posts in one niche gave dozens of requests.

The same held for a platform sold to crypto and AI projects (community
scoring, quests, adoption). A week across seven networks, exact phrases:
in Telegram, X, LinkedIn, Threads, Bluesky and Hacker News the matches
were sellers, reward farmers replying to campaigns, and consultants
posting advice - no project owner asking. Only chosen subreddits held
real owners, one or two posts a week per pain. Was: plan a signal feed
for such an offer. Now: build the list from dated public events
(a protocol listed, a round, a launch) and keep subreddits as colour.

Where people do ask in public, a raw keyword count still overstates
demand by two orders. A week of a marketing service in one market's
Telegram chats and Threads, two languages: about 1,600 matches on the
service words, 72 posts that asked for something, about 10 businesses
actually looking for a contractor. The rest was ad-account resale for
traffic arbitrage, vacancies for a neighbouring market's ad platform,
and competitors' own posts. Was: quote the match count as demand. Now:
read a sample by hand, quote the buyers per week, and build the stop
list from what the noise turned out to be (platform names of the other
market, its currency, "accounts", "vacancy", "salary"). A newer
service the buyers cannot name yet (being recommended by AI assistants)
had zero requests: sell it with a check in the first message, not by
listening.

A post that names what you sell can still come from the wrong buyer.
Measured for an outsourced-sales offer to IT service companies: "looking
for a sales partner" returned 13 posts in 30 days on Reddit and X, nearly
all tiny agencies and freelancers wanting a commission-only closer - people
who will not pay a monthly fee. A first sales job ad was weaker still:
every competitor sees it. The pain sat elsewhere: outsourcing companies
post their bench, the developers sitting between projects, listed by
stack, in outstaff chats - 137 such posts in two weeks in three Telegram
chats. A company with an idle bench pays salaries without revenue and
needs clients this month. Was: a ping named "founders looking for a sales
partner" as the reason to talk. Now: probe the signal before it goes into
a message, read who posts it, and for anyone selling clients or sales to
IT outsourcers start from the bench posts, dropping the markets you do not
serve.

## What cannot be verified

Funding rounds outside the companies a public database covers, headcount
growth, revenue, and what people post are not verifiable at collection
time. A condition like that does not go into a list's filters, and it
does not go into the copy either: a message standing on "I see you are
growing" that nobody checked is an invented fact, and the reader can tell.

## App stores as a source

A company's app is a public, dated record: its rating, how many people
rated it, when it was last updated. That makes a bad rating or an
abandoned app an anchor B you can check for every lead. What was learned
building such lists:

- **The App Store publishes no download counts.** Not in Apple's search
  API, not on the app page; only the app's owner sees them. Measure size
  by the number of ratings in that country instead. Google Play shows
  both, and on business apps there the ratio holds roughly: 10,000+
  installs sit around 200 ratings, 50,000+ around 700, 100,000+ around
  1,900. So 200 / 700 / 1,500 ratings on the App Store stand for about
  the same audience as 10k / 50k / 100k installs. A floor of 1,500 ratings
  looks modest and cuts the list to its biggest apps.
- **Without a size floor, "not updated for months" is mostly apps nobody
  uses.** On the App Store nine in ten such apps had fewer than 200
  ratings, the level of about 10,000 downloads.
- **A rating belongs to one country - the recipient's.** Both stores show
  a different score in each country. The rating, the country and the
  comparison in the message come from the storefront of the company's own
  country, never from the storefront where the search happened to find the
  app. Was: the score came from wherever the app turned up, often the US
  store, so a local app with a 4.6 at home was told it sat at 2-3 stars,
  and about a quarter of a "bad rating" list turned out to be rated 3.5+
  at home. Now: re-read the score in the home storefront before the list
  is built and again on the day of sending. That read is free: the App
  Store lookup takes a country, and the Google Play page takes one too.
  With fewer than 30 ratings in that country, leave the number out.
- **Keep the app's store id and storefront with the lead.** Without them
  a later re-check means finding every app in the store again by name.
- **Keep the domain the person was matched by, and check it before the
  letter.** A list of companies is matched to people through company pages,
  and a company in someone's profile can share the name and not be the
  listed one. Was: a listed cinema chain matched three founders of a
  same-named video platform; their letter would have been about the
  cinema's app. Now: the lead carries the listed domain, the app's seller
  site must be that domain (or the seller name that company's), and a
  mismatch leaves the person out.
- **The app name in a letter is the brand people say, not the store title.**
  Store titles carry taglines and keywords. Cut after " - ", " | ", ": "
  or ", ", drop words like "Official", "App" or a local "New", and write the
  brand the way the company writes it on its site. Was: listing titles such
  as "<brand> Isle of Wight Ferry" or "<brand> Billetes tren y AVE" were one
  step from going into "I noticed {{app}} is at 2.8"; now every app name is
  read in the rendered first message before the list goes in.
- **About a third of what passes a store filter is a buyer.** The rest are
  big brands, publishers from other regions, apps with no business behind
  them and public bodies. Plan the list size from the judged share, not
  from the raw count.
- **A white-label vendor rarely has a badly rated fleet.** Its client apps
  each have too few ratings for the score to mean anything. What a vendor
  fleet does show is its build dates: many of its client apps fall behind
  a platform deadline together.

## Websites as a source

For an offer that fixes a site - a rebuild, speed, search, ads, presence in
AI answers - the prospect's own site is the anchor B, and most of it can be
checked before anyone is written to. Measured on one market's online shops:

- **Detect the platform on the live site, not from a technology database.**
  A database lists domains by platform cheaply, but two thirds of its rows
  were last crawled years ago. Open CMSes show in the page code; a hosted
  platform that blocks bots shows in DNS instead - its shops share a few
  hosting addresses. The database missed that hosted platform entirely.
- **"Old platform" without a size floor is mostly tiny shops.** Of 100
  shops on one open platform, 30 had an estimated 1,000+ search visits a
  month. Put a traffic estimate on every domain before judging it.
- **Speed is a lab test per page, and about one site in four blocks it.**
  Among the rest most were slow on mobile, so slowness alone selects
  almost everyone; it becomes a reason to write only next to real traffic.
- **Who runs ads now is public.** The ads transparency record shows the
  advertiser's ads with the date last shown - a dated anchor for anyone
  selling paid search.
- **AI answers: compare who is cited with who ranks.** For a niche buying
  query, take the top 10 organic results and the domains the search
  engine's AI summary cites. Ranked but not cited is a checkable gap; the
  summary appeared even on brand queries.
- **Finding the owner is the weak step.** Mid-size online shops were rarely
  on LinkedIn: a name search found a real head for about 2 of 15, and the
  site named its legal entity for about 2 of 10. The register then gave
  the head for 8 of 15, but only 1 of those 8 had a LinkedIn profile. When
  the channel is LinkedIn, start from companies that have a LinkedIn page
  and staff on it, then check their sites - not the other way round.
- **The strongest pair is "pays for ads, the page is slow".** Among 33
  service and retail companies taken from LinkedIn, 11 had ads shown in the
  last month, and 5 of those loaded in 8 to 57 seconds on mobile: their ad
  money lands on a page people leave. It is a reason to write for a site
  rebuild and for a paid-search fix alike.
- **"Every site running ad network X" needs no paid tech database.** Was:
  "this needs a technology-lookup subscription". Now: ad networks publish
  a `sellers.json` file listing the publishers they pay, and each publisher
  site lists its networks in its own `/ads.txt`. For two native-ad networks
  the files named about 3,100 and 1,000 owning publishers, free. Owners
  come from `sellers.json` (often a group that runs many sites), single
  sites from scanning `ads.txt` over a domain list, and the page code
  confirms the widget is live. This finds who shows the network's ads, not
  who buys them.
- **"Sells on a marketplace" is not on the homepage.** Was: read the
  brand's front page for an Amazon link. Of 23 consumer brands checked,
  2 linked to Amazon, while a marketplace search for the brand name
  (storefront, or a listing whose title starts with the brand) found 35
  of 49 selling there. Search the marketplace for the brand, drop hits
  that only share a word (music tracks, other brands), and keep the top
  listing's title: it is the real product the first message names.

## Ad networks' public files as a source

Every ad network publishes `sellers.json` (who it pays) and every site that
sells ads publishes `ads.txt` (who may sell its inventory). Together they
answer "which content sites work with network X and not with network Y".

- **Exclude by ads.txt, not by sellers.json alone.** A site missing from a
  network's own sellers.json can still sell through that network via a
  reseller. Was: a list of competitors' publishers minus the network's
  sellers.json, about 4,200 domains. In a sample of 600, a third of the
  live ads.txt files already carried the network through a reseller. Now:
  check the site's ads.txt for the network's line; a site that has it
  through a reseller gets a different message (direct terms), not "join us".
- **The owner is usually in the `name` field.** sellers.json often names
  the person or the company that is paid; one search on that name finds
  the owner's LinkedIn faster than the site's about page. German sites
  name the owner in the Impressum.
- **Group domains by owner before writing.** Several owners in one sample
  ran from two to dozens of similar sites; one conversation covers the
  whole network, and two letters to the same owner from two domains look
  careless.
- **Language is not geography.** Many English-language content sites serve
  India, Nigeria or Ghana; an English page alone does not mean US or UK
  traffic. This was the largest single drop in the funnel (from 292 sites
  without the network to 131 in English or German, 28 strict fits).
- **Facebook pages, not fan groups.** Publishers who buy audience on
  Facebook post links from their own pages and groups. Fan groups found by
  topic were people talking, with almost no outbound links (5 links in 60
  posts). A page's public card names its site and often an email; a large
  page with no site is an audience owner without a site of their own.
