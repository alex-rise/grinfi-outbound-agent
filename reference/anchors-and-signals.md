# Anchors and signals

What a first sentence may stand on, and which events count as a reason to
write now. The strategist assigns one anchor per campaign; the copywriter
writes to it; the audit reads a campaign by it.

## The three anchors

| Anchor | Stands on | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| **A, signal** | an event with a date | the tier 1 and tier 2 lists below |
| **B, data point** | a verifiable fact about the company now, plus what you compare it to | careers page, headcount, tech stack, public metrics |
| **C, segment insight** | a pattern true of a narrow segment, not of this company | knowing the segment |

Anchor B alone says nothing: "you have 8 engineers" is a database row.
The formula is the data point, the comparison, and what it usually means.

Anchor C is the default for most of any list and a legitimate track of
its own. The test: if the opening line could be pasted unchanged into a
message to the reader's direct competitor, the segment is too wide.

Never invent an anchor. No event and no verifiable fact means the message
goes on anchor C and says so. One campaign carries one anchor: in a mixed
campaign the signal contacts pull the average up and hide a segment track
that returns nothing.

## Signal tiers

**Tier 1, high intent.** An open SDR, BDR or sales role, usable about 30
days. A funding round, 30 to 90 days. A new decision maker in the role,
best inside 30 days. Headcount growing more than 20% a quarter. The
decision maker posted about the problem you solve, 24 to 72 hours.

**Tier 2, medium.** A key tool changed, 1 to 3 months. The decision maker
changed jobs 3 to 12 months ago. A competitor is their customer. They
spoke at a conference on your topic. They won a tender.

**Tier 3.** Firmographic fit only. A track of its own, never a filler
mixed into a signal campaign.

## The hiring question

Ask "who is being hired?" before calling a vacancy a signal. It is a
signal only when they are hiring the buyer. An agency hiring a PPC
specialist is building in-house; a company hiring a Head of Marketing is
about to rewrite its strategy.

A role open ninety days or more is a different thing: a stuck hire. That
is anchor B, a data point with a comparison, not a tier 1 signal.

Write to the person who decided to hire, not to the person who posted the
vacancy. Job posts in chats and boards are usually published by a recruiter,
often a contractor, who neither buys nor knows who owns the budget. Was:
reply to the recruiter under the post. Now: name the company (an application
form or a careers link often gives it away when the post does not), find the
founder or head of sales, and write to them with the vacancy as the reason.
If you sell the work that role does, offer both paths in one message: do it
with you, or help them hire well. Someone already hiring will not drop the
plan because a vendor asked.

## Public requests as a source

"Show me who asked for this last week" is a fair ask, but for a specialist
service sold to companies, public posts rarely answer it. Measured over one
week for a marketplace-advertising agency, using exact phrases and no AI
filter, on X (268 posts), Threads (195), the seller communities on Reddit
(about 2,400 posts and comments) and LinkedIn post search: zero companies
in the target countries wrote "we are looking for an agency or specialist".
What matched instead were freelancers advertising themselves, agencies
advertising, and agencies hiring staff.

The buyers were on the job boards. One page of last week's job postings for
the same skill in one country listed about nine brands hiring that role in
house, against five agencies. A brand opening that role has the budget and
the gap right now, and the hiring question above applies: drop the agencies
and recruiters, then write to the person who owns the hire.

Was: search social posts first and report what turned up. Now: for a
company-to-company service, start from brands posting the role on job
boards and use social posts as colour. Social search earns its place where
people do ask in public: freelance work in local-language communities, where
a week of posts in one niche gave dozens of requests.

## What cannot be verified

Funding rounds outside the companies a public database covers, headcount
growth, revenue, and what people post are not verifiable at collection
time. A condition like that does not go into a list's filters, and it
does not go into the copy either: a message standing on "I see you are
growing" that nobody checked is an invented fact, and the reader can tell.

## App stores as a source

A company's app is a public, dated record: its rating, how many people
rated it, when it was last updated. That makes a bad rating or an
abandoned app an anchor B you can check for every lead. What was learned
building such lists:

- **The App Store publishes no download counts.** Not in Apple's search
  API, not on the app page; only the app's owner sees them. Measure size
  by the number of ratings in that country instead. Google Play shows
  both, and on business apps there the ratio holds roughly: 10,000+
  installs sit around 200 ratings, 50,000+ around 700, 100,000+ around
  1,900. So 200 / 700 / 1,500 ratings on the App Store stand for about
  the same audience as 10k / 50k / 100k installs. A floor of 1,500 ratings
  looks modest and cuts the list to its biggest apps.
- **Without a size floor, "not updated for months" is mostly apps nobody
  uses.** On the App Store nine in ten such apps had fewer than 200
  ratings, the level of about 10,000 downloads.
- **A rating belongs to one country.** Google Play shows a different score
  in each country, so the rating, the country and the comparison in the
  message come from the same storefront.
- **About a third of what passes a store filter is a buyer.** The rest are
  big brands, publishers from other regions, apps with no business behind
  them and public bodies. Plan the list size from the judged share, not
  from the raw count.
- **A white-label vendor rarely has a badly rated fleet.** Its client apps
  each have too few ratings for the score to mean anything. What a vendor
  fleet does show is its build dates: many of its client apps fall behind
  a platform deadline together.

## Websites as a source

For an offer that fixes a site - a rebuild, speed, search, ads, presence in
AI answers - the prospect's own site is the anchor B, and most of it can be
checked before anyone is written to. Measured on one market's online shops:

- **Detect the platform on the live site, not from a technology database.**
  A database lists domains by platform cheaply, but two thirds of its rows
  were last crawled years ago. Open CMSes show in the page code; a hosted
  platform that blocks bots shows in DNS instead - its shops share a few
  hosting addresses. The database missed that hosted platform entirely.
- **"Old platform" without a size floor is mostly tiny shops.** Of 100
  shops on one open platform, 30 had an estimated 1,000+ search visits a
  month. Put a traffic estimate on every domain before judging it.
- **Speed is a lab test per page, and about one site in four blocks it.**
  Among the rest most were slow on mobile, so slowness alone selects
  almost everyone; it becomes a reason to write only next to real traffic.
- **Who runs ads now is public.** The ads transparency record shows the
  advertiser's ads with the date last shown - a dated anchor for anyone
  selling paid search.
- **AI answers: compare who is cited with who ranks.** For a niche buying
  query, take the top 10 organic results and the domains the search
  engine's AI summary cites. Ranked but not cited is a checkable gap; the
  summary appeared even on brand queries.
- **Finding the owner is the weak step.** Mid-size online shops were rarely
  on LinkedIn: a name search found a real head for about 2 of 15, and the
  site named its legal entity for about 2 of 10. The register then gave
  the head for 8 of 15, but only 1 of those 8 had a LinkedIn profile. When
  the channel is LinkedIn, start from companies that have a LinkedIn page
  and staff on it, then check their sites - not the other way round.
- **The strongest pair is "pays for ads, the page is slow".** Among 33
  service and retail companies taken from LinkedIn, 11 had ads shown in the
  last month, and 5 of those loaded in 8 to 57 seconds on mobile: their ad
  money lands on a page people leave. It is a reason to write for a site
  rebuild and for a paid-search fix alike.
