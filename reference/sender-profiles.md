# Sender profiles: what a lead sees before replying

A lead who gets a message opens the profile before answering. The profile
never sells on its own, but it has to answer three questions in ten
seconds: who is this, how can this person be useful to me, and what does
their business do. This file is the checklist for bringing a sender
profile to that state. It came out of rewriting real profiles for
several businesses and having the owners correct the drafts.

## The rules, each from a correction

1. **The profile describes the whole business, never one campaign's
   offer.** A sequence is one hypothesis for one segment; the profile is
   read by every segment, including the ones next month's sequence will
   target. Corrected from: headlines built around the current sequence's
   angle ("we fix and update live apps"). The angle may appear as one
   bullet in the About; it never leads the headline.
2. **One Experience text for every profile.** The company description in
   the Experience section is identical on every sender profile, real or
   rented, however many there are; only the job title changes. Corrected
   from: two profiles describing the same company in two different
   first-person texts.
3. **Only approved numbers.** Every figure in a headline, About or
   Experience traces to the client's confirmed material. Corrected from:
   three different team sizes, project counts and retention figures
   living on the website, the deck and the profile at the same time. Pick
   one set, and remove the old deck from Featured.
4. **A colon followed by a long list in one sentence reads as machine
   text.** "We take a product through its whole life: discovery, design,
   native and cross-platform build, backend, store launch, and support."
   AI detectors flag it, and so do readers. Corrected into short sentences
   of uneven length with one plain phrase in each: "We do the whole thing,
   not just the coding. Discovery first, so the first release has a point.
   Then design, then the build itself." A contraction or two helps.
5. **Grammar pass after every owner edit.** Owners edit in the document;
   the next version re-reads every line they touched. Corrected from: a
   typo and a double space that went out in a headline after an edit.

## Headline

`Role at Company | what the business does for whom | the difference or the
proof`. Two or three parts, 220 characters at most. The role comes first:
a senior title in the headline raised acceptance on one profile the same
week it changed. No adjectives without proof next to them.

## About

First person on a real person's profile. One line on who is writing and
what makes them worth listening to (an engineer-founder who reads the
code; the person who owns delivery). Then what the business does, for
whom, in plain sentences of uneven length. Then two or three proof points
with numbers the client approved. Then one call to action: "message me
with a link to your app and what bothers you about it" beats "let's
connect". Up to 2,600 characters, and shorter is better.

## Experience

The one shared company text: what the business is, who it works with,
what it does (a short list with a benefit in each line), how to start.
Up to 2,000 characters. The same text on every profile.

## Beyond the text, in this order

| Check | Why |
|---|---|
| Profile language matches the market | A profile whose primary language is not the lead's reads as foreign before a word is read |
| No long dashes, no exclamation marks anywhere | The long dash is the loudest sign a machine wrote it |
| No open parallel roles | A CEO still listed as a team lead elsewhere reads as an employee with a side business |
| One location across the profile and the current role | Two countries on one page raise a question the lead will not ask, just close the tab |
| Skills: the five that match what is sold, endorsed | The senders endorse each other's top five; three to five colleagues endorse the same |
| Recommendations: from each other and from clients | Senders write one for each other (a reliable partner, delivers what was agreed, on time). Clients who already gave a review on the website are asked to post it as a recommendation, with a draft they can edit |
| Featured: the case study, how to start, the website | Remove anything with figures the client no longer stands behind |
| Website button and the Services section on | Free visibility LinkedIn gives to profiles that list services |
| Forbidden cases and names absent | A case under NDA or a name the client asked not to use does not appear, even in an old About |

## How to hand it over

One document per client: the texts per profile, the shared Experience
text, the fix list beyond the text, and the recommendation drafts. The
owner edits in the document; the next version starts from their edits,
not from the previous draft.
