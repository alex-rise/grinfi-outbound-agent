# Owner rules: 38 corrections from real drafts

Corrections made by the people whose name was under the text, across
several businesses, on drafts that had already passed every other check
in this kit. They are rules, not taste.

They are also not a template. Every sequence is written for one business,
one offer and one reader. When a rule and the situation pull apart, the
situation wins and the reason goes under the draft, so the next reviewer
sees why.

**Read this file before writing message 1 for a new business, and again
before a rewrite.** In a session where copy has already been reviewed
once, the corrections in `business/rules.md` come first: they are this
user's, these are everyone's.

## Who owns the problem

1. Write "we help you build", not "we do it for you". The reader stays
   the owner of the process.
2. Write "we will suggest what can be improved", not "we will tell you
   where the problems are". They did not name a problem to you.
10. Never blame their current vendor, accountant or agency. The limit is
    in the tool or the process, never in the person they hired.
11. Translate a technical consequence into the owner's language: not "a
    missed optimisation" but "money frozen for a year".
20. Do not say "build" to someone who already has the product. Say you
    help improve what they have: the engineering behind it first, the
    user's experience as the result. Do not insist the product is "their
    channel to customers" when for many it is not.
22. Do not show the reader their own small numbers - installs, rating
    count, followers. A small number pushes them away from their own
    product instead of towards fixing it.
35. Never frame the reader as behind ("a project a couple of years
    behind"). State the mechanism in the abstract: "done now it takes a
    day or two; put off until spring it becomes a week or more, because
    each new release drops more of what the current build relies on".

## The ask and the first step

3. The invitation comes from a person: "I invite you to a 30-minute
   call", not "half an hour with the founder".
4. Put "if relevant" before a link.
7. Set the density of the ask by role. A direct client gets the calendar
   link. An expert or a partner gets a spoken ask for a short call; a
   calendar in a first touch reads as pressure between equals.
23. The call is the CTA of a free offer. "Who looks after this on your
    side?" gives you nothing; the call is where a client separates from
    someone who wants the free part and leaves.
24. A free step carries a caveat: "if you let us take a look and clarify
    a few details, we could do it for you free of charge". The promise
    follows the look, never the other way round, which keeps the right to
    decline after qualification. Say "free", not "at cost". Hint softly
    at what comes next ("if there are plans for the product beyond that,
    happy to talk them through") instead of "a first step to something
    bigger".
27. One product per sequence. The add-on gets one phrase and never the
    finale; the lead product is named first and closes the sequence.
29. A demo shows your own live setup; it does not build the client's on
    the call. The invitation promises only what really happens there.
37. Frame a free first step as a trial run of working together: "I'd
    gladly walk you through what needs doing on a call, or do it for you
    free of charge as a trial run of working together". The same framing
    carries into message 2. The CTA stays as it is.

## What may be claimed

5. Give numbers as ranges ("up to 30%"), not as exact terms you will have
   to defend in the first reply.
9. Never attribute to the person something they did not say. The only
   fact about them is the one that put them on the list.
18. Never claim a number you cannot know. "The three complaints that
    repeat most" is a lie if nobody read the reviews. Pick a number large
    enough to land: the share of ratings at three stars or below, not the
    share of one-star ratings, which may be 3% and sound silly. Avoid
    variables that may not exist on the send day - new reviews since the
    last message, a fresh count.
21. Reviews: no verbatim quotes, they run long and read as an exposé. Not
    "your one- and two-star reviews" but "judging by the reviews, users
    are most often unhappy with X and Y". Do not count the fixes ("one or
    two spots"); keep the idea that the cause usually sits on the
    technical side, not in the business.
25. Every claim carries its argument. "A day or two now, a week in
    spring" is cut unless the text says what takes the time and why it
    grows.
26. Never promise a result that depends on things outside your control
    ("let's see how many replies to expect"). Promise only what happens
    on the call or after the click.
32. A technical company reads as technical. If the reader could take the
    message for reputation management, marketing or consulting, the
    second sentence names what the team actually does: "a mobile
    development team, we fix, rework and extend apps that are already
    live". Offer to fix what users complain about; the rating, the
    retention and the reviews are consequences, never the promise. Do not
    swap cause and effect.

## The shape of the text

8. Kill tautologies: the same root in neighbouring sentences means change
   the verb, not the paragraph.
13. Literary is unnatural. No aphorisms, no metaphors, no stories. Plain
    business language: who we are, what we do, what you get, a question.
14. Sell the difference, not the list of services. "Bookkeeping, payroll,
    sales tax" is every accountant; who answers, what you do yourselves,
    the packaged price, the personal alongside the business is one firm.
15. Open softly and like a person: "I noticed your app sits at 3.1 while
    similar ones sit around 4.2". Not a dry analyst's report, not a punch
    at the pain. Cut the sentence that explains their pain back to them
    ("that is the number every new customer sees"): they know.
16. Write no exit paths into your own text. "Whether we work together or
    not", "you keep it either way" attract people who collect free work
    and leave. The deliverable does stay with them; present it as what
    they get from the call, not as a door out.
28. A product message is two short paragraphs by function, two or three
    benefits in each, no lists, and nothing the reader will see on the
    demo anyway.
34. Shorten by cutting explanations and examples, not meaning. "No longer
    offered to new users on new phones, so an audience of N installs has
    quietly stopped growing" becomes "no longer offered to users on new
    versions, which slows down installs". A first message rarely needs
    more than 450 characters.

## Follow-ups and the close

6. Use social proof instead of instruction: "many agencies use this for
   their own clients", not "you could earn in two ways".
12. Make the closing question open, about their plans or how their
    process works. "Are you planning big decisions this year?" is closed,
    and "no" ends the dialogue.
17. A follow-up restates in one line who you are and why you are writing.
    The reader does not remember message 1 and may never have opened it.
    Greet by name in follow-ups too.
19. The breakup does not ask for a referral: nobody gives one to a
    stranger. It reveals something else that may be relevant, the full
    range of what the business does, and offers to talk if there is a
    challenge on their side. Give the reader options; do not decide for
    them what they care about.
30. Greet in every message, the breakup included.
33. Use no worn sales phrases: "one more thing", "quick reminder", "as
    this is a follow-up", "following up from", "just circling back".
    Reminding someone who you are means saying what business you are and
    how you are useful, not telling the reader this is a follow-up. A
    follow-up continues the thought: "I wanted to add one thing to the
    point about X".
36. Message 2 brings new detail or new value, not the same pain with a
    different metric. "Your rating is low, let me tell you what to do"
    twice over is a repeat even with a fresh number. Dig the new angle
    out of the business's own materials: platform rules and thresholds,
    deadlines, whatever costs the reader users beyond what message 1
    already named.
38. Take time pressure from the platform's deadline, never from your own
    offer expiring. Not "free until 1 November" but "starting now still
    leaves time to make the store's deadline".

## How copy is reviewed

31. Review with the owner one message at a time, in sequence order. Four
    versions in a row cannot be read, and a correction to message 1
    arrives before message 2 is shown.
